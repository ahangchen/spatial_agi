PUGS: Physics-Guided Uncertainty Modeling for Underwater
Dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting
Tingjun Li, Yuxi Wang∗, Guoqiang Zhong∗,Member, IEEE, Zhaoxiang Zhang,Senior Member, IEEE
.
Abstract—Dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has recently
emerged as an efficient paradigm for reconstructing dynamic
scenes. Existing approaches predominantly model scene dynamics
through deterministic deformation fields, implicitly assuming
that each Gaussian follows a unique geometric trajectory over
time. However, this assumption fundamentally breaks down in
underwater scenes, where light scattering, refraction, marine
snow, and severe visibility degradation introduce substantial
physical ambiguity into the imaging process. To address this
problem, we present a physics-guided uncertainty model for
underwater dynamic 3DGS (PUGS) that learns to detect the
inherent uncertainty induced by underwater image formation
and exploits it as a physical signal to guide dynamic scene
representation. Instead of estimating a single deterministic dis-
placement, PUGS parameterizes each Gaussian’s motion as
a probability distribution characterized by both its expected
deformation and physical uncertainty. Specifically, we first in-
troduce a stochastic deformation optimization strategy that
samples Gaussian trajectories through local reparameterization,
encouraging adaptive exploration of multiple physically plausible
solutions during training. To progressively consolidate the learned
geometry, we further propose an uncertainty stochastic annealing
framework together with a dual-path self-consistency distillation
objective, which gradually transforms stochastic exploration into
deterministic reconstruction while preserving temporal coher-
ence. Extensive experiments on challenging underwater dynamic
datasets and physics-aware benchmark demonstrate that the pro-
posed method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods
in rendering quality, temporal consistency, and reconstruction
robustness under complex conditions, achieving an average PSNR
improvement of 1.16 dB over the strongest baseline on the
UVEB benchmark and 3.99 dB on DRUV A. Project page is that:
https://tingtingpius.github.io/PUGS/.

this, dynamic extensions such as Deformable 3DGS [4] and

change as a deterministic coordinate shift, assuming that all
temporal variations correspond to meaningful structural mo-
tion. However, in underwater scenarios, marine snow [26] and
refractive caustics introduce stochastic perturbations whose

In this paper, we posit thatthe central challenge of un-
derwater dynamic reconstruction is not merely recovering
geometry from degraded observations, but learning to de-
tect and exploit the physical uncertainty signal embedded
in the imaging process.This signal, once extracted, reveals
which scene regions are affected by scattering, refraction,
or turbidity—enabling the reconstruction to adaptively allo-
cate representational capacity and to disentangle persistent
structural motion from transient optical interference. Rather
than forcing every observation to correspond to a unique
deformation, an ideal representation should distinguish persis-

allowing multiple physically plausible hypotheses during op-
timization before converging to a consistent reconstruction.
Motivated by this observation, we propose a Physics-Guided
Uncertainty Model for Underwater Dynamic 3D Gaussian
Splatting, called PUGS. Instead of predicting deterministic
Gaussian displacements, PUGS reformulates dynamic scene
representation from a probabilistic perspective, modeling each
Gaussian trajectory as a latent distribution characterized by its
expected motion and physical uncertainty. Here, uncertainty
is not treated as an auxiliary confidence score, but serves
as a compact representation of the stochastic physical effects
introduced by underwater image formation. Consequently, the
model naturally separates stable structural evolution from tran-
sient optical disturbances, yielding significantly more robust
deformation estimation in visually degraded environments.

develop a unified uncertainty-aware optimization framework
consisting of three complementary components. First, we
introduce stochastic Gaussian deformation via local reparame-
terization, enabling adaptive exploration of multiple physically

II. RELATEDWORK
A. Dynamic Scene Reconstruction
Early neural approaches for dynamic scene reconstruction,
such as D-NeRF [8] and Nerfies [9], extended NeRF by
learning deformation fields to map time-varying observations
to a canonical space, though their purely MLP-based nature in-
curred significant computational costs. Subsequent hybrid rep-
resentations like TiNeuV ox [30] and HexPlane [15] improved
efficiency through voxel grids and factorized spatiotemporal
volumes. Beyond NeRF-based paradigms, a diverse body
of work has explored alternative representations. Traditional
methods leverage multi-view stereo and SLAM systems [31]
to reconstruct dynamic scenes through mesh deformation
and keyframe-based optimization. More recently, learning-
based approaches emerge, including video segmentation-based
techniques that treat dynamic reconstruction as layer de-
composition [32], and depth-prior methods that incorporate
monocular depth estimates to regularize the under-constrained
problem [33]. These methods, while effective in specific
domains, often rely on strong priors or suffer from limited
generalizability.
With the advent of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1], the
field shifts toward explicit scene representations that achieve
real-time rendering with high fidelity. Dynamic extensions of
3DGS rapidly proliferate. Deformable-3DGS [4] employs a
deformation network to predict time-dependent transforma-
tions per Gaussian, while 4D-GS [5] adopts HexPlane-based
spatiotemporal structures for efficient optimization. Gaussian-
Flow [6] integrates optical flow supervision, and SC-GS [34]
introduces sparse control points to reduce computational over-
head. SAGD [40] further decomposes boundary Gaussians
to enforce geometric structure, mitigating the ambiguity of
unconstrained 3DGS primitives. Other notable approaches
include Spacetime-GS [35] and 3DGStream [36], which fo-
cus on streaming and long-sequence reconstruction. Collec-
tively, these methods [59], [71] establish deformation-field-
based 3DGS as the prevailing paradigm for dynamic recon-
struction. Critically, however, all of these methods inherit a
shared assumption: that every observed pixel change originates
from geometric motion. In underwater environments, where
scattering, refraction, and marine snow produce pixel-level
fluctuations that are statistically structured by the physical
properties of the water medium, this assumption becomes
untenable—the models cannot distinguish a floating particle
from a moving surface, because they lack any mechanism to
detect the physical signal embedded in these degradations.
Moreover, none of the approaches above incorporate mech-
anisms to represent or reason about motion uncertainty [60].
while methods such as Shape of Motion [2] and Mosca
[3] introduce new physical prior constraints such as depth
maps, 2D tracks, masks, etc. to achieve high-quality motion
information and geometric depth estimation, they neglect
the reconstruction quality. Our method requires no external
supervision signals such as depth maps, optical flow, or 2D
tracks—it relies solely on the learned physical uncertainty
signal to guide reconstruction. PUGS introduces a variational
Bayesian deformation framework that learns to detect this
physical signal, enabling robust underwater reconstruction
by decoupling structural evolution from stochastic medium
interference.
B. Uncertainty Quantification in Rendering
The survey by Wang et al. [72] provides comprehen-
sive coverage of existing methods, but it focuses primarily
on uncertainty in 2D vision tasks, where uncertainty-aware
pseudo-label refinement [74] and entropy-based curriculum
self-training [75] have been extensively studied; in dynamic
3D reconstruction, deformation-level uncertainty remains an
open gap. Uncertainty estimation is essential for deploy-
ing neural rendering methods in safety-critical applications.
Existing uncertainty quantification approaches generally fall
into three categories: closed-form likelihood models, Bayesian
methods, and ensemble methods. Closed-form likelihood mod-
els, exemplified by NeRF-W [17] and ActiveNeRF [12], model
ray colors using probabilistic distributions to handle transient

With the emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting, recent work
begins exploring uncertainty quantification within this explicit
paradigm. Methods like horseshoe Splatting [38], [61]intro-
duce a Bayesian extension of 3DGS with factorized vari-
ational inference for calibrated uncertainty estimation [62].
RU4D-SLAM [39] incorporates uncertainty-aware perception
into Gaussian Splatting SLAM for dynamic scenes through
semantic-guided reweighting. More relevant to dynamic recon-
struction, FUGS [28] and RobustSplat [29] explicitly models
motion uncertainty by learning uncertainty relations among
neighboring Gaussians. Additionally, FisherRF [41] takes an
information-theoretic approach for downstream tasks such as
view selection [63].
However, the aforementioned methods [68] share a common
characteristic: they treat uncertainty as a confidence score—a
scalar weight that modulates the contribution of each pixel
or Gaussian to the reconstruction loss. While effective for
outlier rejection, this passive formulation does not capture
where or why the uncertainty arises, nor does it use un-
certainty to actively guide the reconstruction process. PUGS
takes a fundamentally different approach. Rather than treating
uncertainty as a post-hoc confidence estimate, we treat it as
a physical signal. The position variance∆σpredicted by
our variational Bayesian deformation field is not an abstract
confidence measure—it is a learned detector that responds to
the statistical signatures of underwater optical degradation.
When scattering or refractive distortion dominates a scene
region,∆σincreases, signaling that the model should ex-
pand its exploration radius and allocate additional Gaussian
capacity. When the water is clear and observations are reliable,
∆σattenuates, and the model converges to near-deterministic
behavior. This active sensing-and-guidance loop—where the
physical signal is first detected and then exploited for ex-
ploration, refinement, and densification—distinguishes PUGS
from all existing uncertainty-aware rendering methods.
C. Underwater Vision and Reconstruction
Underwater imaging is fundamentally constrained by the
physical properties of the aquatic medium [42], [64]. As de-
scribed by the Jaffe-McGlamery model [48], light propagation
underwater is governed by wavelength-dependent absorption
5
and forward/backward scattering, which collectively degrade
image quality through color distortion, contrast reduction,
and haze-like artifacts [44]. Traditional restoration methods
focused on 2D image enhancement, employing hand-crafted
priors such as the Dark Channel Prior (DCP) [43] to com-
pensate for these degradations through post-processing tech-
niques. While effective for improving visual quality, these 2D
approaches lack any notion of 3D geometry, limiting their
applicability to tasks requiring spatial understanding.
The transition to 3D reconstruction in underwater envi-
ronments is driven by integrating medium-specific physical
models into neural rendering frameworks. WaterNeRF [18]
first extends NeRF to underwater scenes by incorporating
attenuation coefficients into the volume rendering pipeline,
enabling joint estimation of scene geometry and water pa-
rameters from monocular video. SeaThru-NeRF [13] further
advances this direction by modeling both absorption and
scattering effects, achieving more accurate color restoration
and geometry reconstruction in turbid conditions. These works
[65], [66] demonstrate that implicitly accounting for the under-
water medium is essential for high-fidelity 3D reconstruction,
yet they remain confined to static scenes.
Extending underwater reconstruction to dynamic 4D sce-
narios: capturing marine life, moving AUVs, or time-varying
water conditions, introduces additional complexity. In dynamic
settings, the coupling of fluid dynamics with optical distortions
makes it difficult to distinguish genuine scene motion from
medium-induced artifacts: a floating particle and a moving
fish scale can produce nearly identical pixel-level changes
under refractive distortion. Existing dynamic reconstruction
frameworks, which assume deterministic motion models, often
misinterpret refractive fluctuations and suspended particles
as geometric changes, leading to ”geometric bleeding” and
temporal incoherence. To our knowledge, PUGS is the first
framework to address this challenge by learning to detect the
physical signal [67] within a Gaussian Splatting paradigm,
decoupling true structural motion from stochastic medium
interference.
III. METHOD
In this section, we present PUGS for high-fidelity dynamic
scene reconstruction grounded in a physical uncertainty strat-
egy, with a particular focus on challenging scenarios such
as underwater environments. To address the susceptibility of
deterministic deformation fields to local minima and ”float-
ing” artifacts, we formulate uncertainty-aware physics-based
motion modeling within a variational inference paradigm.
As illustrated in Figure 2, our method consists of three
tightly coupled components:(1) Uncertainty-Guided Dy-
namic Scene Representation.While maintaining a set
of static canonical Gaussians as the geometric scaffold, we
introduce a stochastic variational deformation field to model
per-frame motion. Instead of predicting a single deterministic
displacement, the field simultaneously predicts a mean and
a per-Gaussian position variance, treating the variance as a
representation of uncertainty and thereby encoding physical
uncertainty as a learnable distribution.(2) Uncertainty-
Guided Adaptive Refinement with Annealing.The learned
position variance not only serves as an indicator for trig-
gering adaptive Gaussian splitting and densification in high-
uncertainty regions, but is also multiplied by the annealing
factorβ(t), progressively diminishing its own contribution,
ensuring that refinement operates on increasingly stable ge-
ometry as training converges.(3) Dual-Path Rendering with
Self-Consistent Distillation.During training, we construct a
uncertainty-aware rendering path via reparameterization and a
parallel deterministic path using only the mean displacement.
A self-consistency distillation loss aligns the deterministic
prediction with the visual consensus of the uncertainty path,
encouraging the deterministic output to capture the central
tendency of the explored distribution.
A. Uncertainty-Guided Dynamic Scene Representation
In underwater dynamic scenes, the observed pixel inten-
sity at the camera is not a deterministic function of 3D
geometry alone. Rather, it is the resultant of three interacting
optical pathways: direct transmission, forward scattering, and
backscatter [13]. The latter two components introduce stochas-
tic fluctuations whose magnitude depends on the local density
of suspended particles, the optical properties of the water
column, and the relative geometry among the light source,
the scene, and the camera. Critically, these fluctuations exhibit
strong spatial structure: regions with high turbidity or dense
particle concentrations produce substantially larger frame-
to-frame variance than regions in clear water. This makes
themagnitudeof optical degradation a detectable physical
quantity.
Our core idea is to learn this quantity. Rather than modeling
the forward physics of underwater image formation explicitly,
we train a neural deformation field to predict, alongside
each Gaussian’s expected displacement, a position variance
∆σthat captures the local magnitude of physically induced
observational ambiguity. When∆σis large, it signals that the
corresponding image region is likely affected by scattering,
refraction, or particulate occlusion—the model is, in effect,
learning to sense the underwater optical environmentthrough
the statistics of its own reconstruction uncertainty. This learned
physical signal subsequently drives two complementary pro-
cesses: stochastic exploration with progressive annealing and
uncertainty-guided Gaussian densification.

view-dependent colorcencoded via spherical harmonics. The
rendering process projects these Gaussians onto the image
plane via differentiable splatting. For a given pixelp, the
rendered colorC(p)is computed as

This traditional formulation [4] allows the network to learn
smooth and plausible motions while maintaining a compact

struggle with ambiguous scenarios such as rapid motion,
optical distortions, or occlusions. These ambiguities manifest
as optimization challenges, trapping the learning process in
suboptimal local minima and resulting in characteristic arti-
facts like floating Gaussians or geometric inconsistencies. This
issue is especially severe in underwater scenes, where complex
physical motions, light refraction, and scattering collectively
degrade the imaging process. We address this fundamental
limitation by reformulating deformation prediction as a prob-
abilistic process, thereby substantially mitigating these motion
ambiguities.
Our deformation networkϕ θ takes as input a canonical
positionx can ∈R 3 and a time embeddinge t ∈R d , and

displacement vector. Specifically, the network predicts both the
mean displacement∆µ∈R 3 and position variance∆σ∈R 3

The mean displacement∆µrepresents the expected motion
trajectory, while the variance∆σquantifies the physical posi-
tion uncertainty at each spatial location and temporal instant.
The variance∆σcan be understood as an adaptive exploration
radius. In regions with clear, unambiguous observations, the
network learns to predict small variances, leading to deter-
ministic, precise positioning. Conversely, in regions exhibiting
physical uncertainty such as motion blur, optical distortions,
or occlusions, larger variances permit broader exploration,
allowing the optimization to discover geometrically consistent
solutions that might be missed by deterministic approaches.
Our formulation uses anℓ 1 uncertainty sparsity loss on
∆σin place of an explicit KL divergence term. This choice
is both pragmatic and physically motivated. A zero-centered
Laplace prior (of whichℓ 1 regularization is the maximum a
posteriori counterpart) encodes the inductive bias that physical
uncertainty should be sparse while only regions affected by
scattering, refraction, or occlusion should exhibit substantial
uncertainty. The uncertainty sparsity penalty thus encourages
the model to allocate its exploration budget exclusively to
regions where the underwater medium has genuinely degraded
the visual signal, preventing variance inflation in clear, unam-
biguous areas.
B. Uncertainty-Guided Adaptive Refinement with Annealing
During training, we employ the reparameterization strategy
to enable stochastic exploration of the deformation space while
maintaining gradient flow. The actual displacement applied to
each Gaussian is sampled as :
∆x= ∆µ+β(t)·(∆σ⊙ϵ),ϵ∼N(0,I),(4)
where⊙denotes element-wise multiplication andβ(t)∈[0,1]
is a global stochastic annealing (SA) factor that controls the
exploration magnitude. This formulation allows Gaussians to
stochastically jitter around their predicted mean positions,
effectively exploring the local optimization landscape. The
key insight is that this stochastic perturbation enables the
optimization to escape sharp local minima [69] that commonly
arise from physically ambiguous observations in challenging
underwater scenarios.
The reparameterized sampling in Eq. (4) can be understood
as a physics-aware exploration mechanism. In regions where
∆σis large the stochastic perturbation automatically expands
the exploration radius, enabling the optimization to evaluate
multiple physically plausible configurations. In clear, well-
observed regions where∆σis small, the perturbation naturally
shrinks, and the deformation behaves near-deterministically.
The annealing factorβ(t)globally modulates this behavior:
early in training, it permits broad exploration to escape poor
local minima induced by optical ambiguity; as training con-
verges, it suppresses perturbation, allowing the model to settle
into a temporally coherent reconstruction guided by the now-
stabilized physical signal.
Furthermore, traditional Gaussian splitting strategies con-
sider only the spatial extent of Gaussian primitives. We extend
this criterion by treating the learned physical uncertainty∆σ
as an explicit signal for adaptive capacity allocation. When
a Gaussian exhibits position variance exceeding a predefined
threshold, it indicates that the underlying scene region is sub-
ject to severe underwater optical degradation—dense scatter-
ing, refractive caustics, or marine snow occlusion—that cannot
be adequately represented at the current spatial resolution.
We therefore split such high-uncertainty Gaussians, allocating
additional primitives to model the complex, medium-induced
appearance variations. This mechanism ensures that repre-
sentational capacity is automatically concentrated where the
physical environment most degrades visual information, rather
than being uniformly distributed across the scene.
C. Dual-Path Rendering with Self-Consistency Distillation
A core contribution of PUGS is theDual-Path Rendering
Self-Consistency Distillationframework, which jointly opti-
mizes stochastic and deterministic rendering branches to en-
force long-term structural coherence while enabling local ex-
ploration to escape poor minima induced by optical ambiguity.
During training, we construct two parallel rendering paths that
share the same underlying Gaussian primitives but differ in
how deformations are applied: a deterministic clean path using
only the predicted mean displacement∆µto produceI clean,
and a uncertainty path using the reparameterized displacement
from Eq. (4) to produceI unc [45]. By supervisingI unc against
the ground-truth frame, we implicitly encourage the model to
converge toward flat minima in the loss landscape—regions
where small perturbations do not catastrophically degrade
reconstruction quality. The reconstruction objective integrates
pixel-wiseℓ 1 loss with D-SSIM [46] to capture both high-
frequency textures and global structures:

A critical challenge is ensuring that the deterministic expec-
tation∆µremains an accurate surrogate for the entire motion
distribution at inference time, when stochastic sampling is
disabled. We resolve this by introducing aself-consistency
distillation lossthat explicitly aligns the clean path output
with the visual consensus formed by the uncertainty path:
Ldistill =∥I clean−sg(I unc)∥1,(6)
where sg(·)denotes the stop-gradient operation. Crucially,
gradients flow exclusively through the clean path, treating the
stochastically explored rendering as a fixed visual target; if
gradients instead propagated through both paths, the optimiza-
tion would collapse the uncertainty path toward the clean pre-
diction, nullifying the exploration benefit. This design forces
the mean displacement to capture the consensus appearance
across the distribution of physically plausible deformations.
Concretely, when marine snow or refractive caustics create
multiple equally valid pixel configurations, the uncertainty
path samples across these alternatives, and the distillation
loss pulls the clean path toward their central tendency. This
mechanism [70] enables PUGS to produce sharp, temporally
coherent renderings at inference time despite stochastic train-
ing perturbations.
To prevent the deformation field from inflating∆σas a
slack variable, we impose anℓ 1 uncertainty sparsity loss on
the uncertainty field, denotedL unc =|∆σ| 1. The complete
objective is formulated as:
Ltotal =L recon +λ distill·L distill +λ unc·L unc.(7)
We employ a dynamic scheduling forλ distill, progressively
shifting the optimization priority from broad stochastic explo-
ration to precise deterministic alignment as training converges,
thereby ensuring that the clean path—and thus inference-
time rendering—inherits the robustness benefits of variational
exploration while ultimately satisfying long-term structural
coherence across the temporal sequence.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Settings
DatasetsWe compare PUGS on three different bench-
marks, including two underwater datasets: UVEB [55] and
DRUV A [51], and a new dataset of complex physical scenes
PhysGaia [50]. UVEB is a diverse set of complex dynamic
underwater scenes. On this dataset, we run experiments on
eleven scenes. These scenes collectively encompass diverse
reconstruction challenges, including fine-grained texture de-
tails (Boat), high-speed non-rigid motion (Snake), articulated
deformations (Fish), complex occlusions (Shoal), and severe
refractive distortions coupled with “marine snow” effects
(Coral), providing a comprehensive testbed for evaluating
dynamic reconstruction under realistic underwater optical con-
ditions. DRUV A is an underwater dataset featuring highly
turbid scenes. In this dataset, we run experiments on four
scenes: A1, A2, A11, and A12. PhysGaia is a new dataset
of complex physical scenes, and we run experiments on seven
scenes: hanok, single flag, pisa, bouncing balls, basin, tube,
and single smoke.
For UVEB and DRUV A, each scene is captured by monoc-
ular video in underwater environments, with camera poses
estimated using COLMAP [10] and formatted to follow the
D-NeRF data structure. Following the official protocol of
Deformable-GS [4], we split each scene into training and
test sets. For PhysGaia, we follow the benchmark protocol
established in PhysGaia [50].
Implementation DetailsAll experiments are conducted on a
single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24GB memory, using
PyTorch [47] as the deep learning framework. For a fair
comparison, we train all baseline methods using their official
implementations with the hyperparameters recommended in
their respective papers, and we unify the training iterations to
40,000 across all methods to ensure consistent optimization
budgets. Each scene takes approximately two hours to train,
including both our method and the baselines. We note that the
original PhysGaia paper reports results with fewer decimal
places than our other two benchmarks. To maintain a consis-
tent presentation precision across all three datasets, we pad the
published baseline values with trailing zeros where necessary.
No values are altered beyond this formatting adjustment.

distractor segmentation masks, achieving better quantitative
and qualitative results than vanilla 3D Gaussian Splatting and
RobustNeRF in cluttered scenes.

Results on DRUV ATable II reports quantitative results on
the DRUV A dataset across four underwater scenes. PUGS
achieves the best PSNR on all four scenes and the best
SSIM on three scenes, demonstrating strong pixel-level re-
construction accuracy. However, we observe a PSNR-LPIPS
trade-off in highly turbid scenes. On A11 and A12, PUGS
attains substantially higher PSNR (37.63 dB and 35.35 dB)
than the next-best method UDF (34.03 dB and 31.01 dB),
yet its LPIPS scores (0.209 and 0.233) lag behind UDF
(0.123 and 0.100). We attribute this to variance inflation in
regions with dense floating particles. The model accounts
for turbidity-induced occlusion by increasing the exploration
radius, which can introduce subtle blurring that penalizes
perceptual metrics while preserving pixel-wise accuracy. We
regard mitigating this trade-off as a direction for future work.

ods, including TiNeuV ox, SeaThru-NeRF, 4DGaussian, 4DGS,
WaterSplatting, and UDF, in overall PSNR and SSIM.

In turbid water, our method exhibits some blurriness: in A11,
the rope behind the stone slab appears slightly blurred, and
in A12, the background behind the stone slab also shows
mild blurring. These artifacts are consistent with the variance
inflation behavior discussed above. Nevertheless, even in these
challenging cases, PUGS preserves sharper foreground struc-
tures compared to baselines.

framework generalizes beyond underwater domains. A per-
scene analysis reveals a pattern consistent with our design
philosophy. PUGS delivers its most decisive gains on scenes

marginal or no benefit, as every pixel-level change corresponds

uncertainty rather than functioning as a generic regularizer,
and suggests that a data-driven annealing policy adapting the
exploration level to scene statistics could further extend PUGS
to broader dynamic scenarios.

(1) Stochastic Annealing Mechanism.As shown in Table IV,
when SA is introduced alone, the model achieves a significant
performance boost, demonstrating that controlled stochasticity
enables broader exploration of the solution space, allowing
the model to escape local minima induced by refractive noise.
However, SA alone yields limited improvement in perceptual
quality. This underscores the necessity of coupling stochastic
exploration with robust regularization to ensure fine-grained
detail recovery. When SA is combined withL sparse, perfor-
mance further improves to PSNR 30.53 dB and LPIPS 0.0995,
indicating that the sparsity constraint effectively prevents the
network from inflating uncertainty arbitrarily while preserving
SA’s exploratory benefits. Similarly, combining SA withL distill
yields PSNR 30.48 dB, showing that the distillation loss helps
enhance temporal coherence.
(2) Uncertainty Sparsity Loss.When applied independently
to the baseline,L sparse yields a moderate improvement in
geometric compactness. This confirms thatL sparse effectively
prevents the network from inflating uncertainty arbitrarily, pro-
moting compact geometry and eliminating floating artifacts.
When combined withL distill, the model achieves PSNR 30.34
dB and LPIPS 0.0990, demonstrating that these two regular-
ization terms work complementarily to enforce both geometric
compactness and temporal stability. The best combination
involvingL sparse appears when paired with SA, where the
exploratory capability of SA and the compactness constraint
jointly contribute to reconstruction.
(3) Self-Consistency Distillation Loss.IncorporatingL distill
independently forces the mean prediction to remain robust
against stochastic perturbations, leading to substantial im-
provements in perceptual quality. Visually, this manifests as
significantly sharper textures and reduced motion blur. When
combined withL sparse, the two regularization terms together
achieve strong performance (PSNR 30.34 dB), demonstrating
their complementary roles in enforcing both temporal coher-
ence and geometric compactness.
V. CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented PUGS, a framework that re-
formulates underwater dynamic scene reconstruction as prob-
abilistic inference over Gaussian trajectories. Unlike deter-
ministic deformation fields that conflate structural motion
with optical perturbations, PUGS explicitly models physical
12
uncertainty as a learnable distribution over per-Gaussian dis-
placements. A variational deformation field jointly predicts
motion means and position variances, enabling reparameter-
ized stochastic sampling that escapes local minima induced
by physically ambiguous observations. Dual-path rendering
couples a stochastic exploratory branch with a deterministic
inference branch via self-consistency distillation and pro-
gressive uncertainty annealing, while the learned variance
guides adaptive Gaussian densification toward optically de-
graded regions. Across three benchmarks spanning 22 dynamic
scenes, PUGS achieves state-of-the-art performance, including
30.85 dB PSNR on UVEB (+1.16 dB over Deformable-GS)
with pronounced gains of +3.74 dB and +3.40 dB on Cave
and Boat, and 34.41 dB on DRUV A (+3.99 dB over UDF).
Limitationsinclude a PSNR–LPIPS trade-off under extreme
turbidity and the reliance on manually scheduled annealing,
both of which motivate future work on data-driven uncertainty
scheduling. Beyond underwater domains, this principled inte-
gration of physical image formation and uncertainty modeling
offers a promising direction for robust reconstruction in fog,
smoke, and other scattering media.
