GreenWAM: A World
Foundation Model for Robotics
Gennady Sidorov, Valerii Kornilov, Denis Grankin, Polina Fedotova, Aleksey Postnikov
Brain Team, Sber Robotics Center
A robot needs a world model that predicts how its actions will change the scene and a policy that chooses
those actions. Models are usually built and evaluated for only one of these roles, and every robot records its
actions in its own format. We ask whether one robot world model can serve both roles across different bodies,
and what makes this work. We present GreenW AM, a world foundation model for robotics, together with the
recipe that produces it: (1) a shared anatomical action space that describes 20 robot families, from bimanual
arms to dexterous humanoids, in common joint coordinates; (2) joint training on these families in visual,
action-conditioned and world–action modes; and (3) policies that split an action expert off the generator.
As a world model, GreenW AMattains top-tier trajectory, depth and 3D accuracy on WorldArena 1.0, ahead
of every world–action model and video generator in our comparison. As a policy, it scores among the top
entries of the RoboTwin 2.0 leaderboard, reaches the highest zero-shot LIBERO-plus success among the
compared methods and is competitive with dedicated vision–language–action and world–action policies on
CAL VIN and LIBERO. We further adapt GreenW AM to a real humanoid, where it succeeds more often
than policies adapted from 𝜋0.5 and from Cosmos 3 Edge, and distill it into a single-step policy for real-time
control. Controlled experiments yield three findings: robot specialization improves world prediction and
control together; an action expert helps when split off at the start of adaptation; and keeping world-model
modes during policy adaptation preserves robustness to scene changes.

1 Introduction
World foundation models learn how scenes evolve from large-scale video, and world–action models reuse this
knowledge to predict observations and actions jointly [ 58, 92]. For a robot, the two uses are linked: the
model must predict how the scene changes when the robot moves, and it must produce that motion itself.
Both uses therefore depend on how the model represents the robot body. Yet models are usually built and
evaluated for one of these roles: world models are judged by their predictions, world–action models by the
success of the resulting policy.
Robot data are fragmented across bodies and recording conventions. Joint layouts, grippers and dexterous
hands, camera sets, frame rates and task boundaries differ between datasets, and the same tensor dimension
can mean different motions on different robots. Current models describe a robot through an action projection
for each embodiment domain, through end-effector poses with gripper or hand states, or through joint vectors
padded to a common length [ 58, 2]. In each case the correspondence between the arms, torso, legs and fingers
of different bodies remains implicit or absent. We ask whether one robot world model can serve both as a
world model and as a policy across different bodies, and what makes this work.
We present GreenW AM, a world foundation model for robotics built on Cosmos 3 Edge [ 58], together
with the recipe that produces it (Figure 1). Cosmos 3 Edge learned robot actions only coarsely: from
six embodiments dominated by a single dataset, as end-effector pose deltas and a gripper opening, with a
separate projection for each embodiment domain (Figure 2). The recipe replaces this in three steps. First, a
shared anatomical action space describes the bodies of 20 robot families, from bimanual arms to humanoids
with dexterous hands, in common joint coordinates: a codec for each robot layout maps its joints onto
shared slots for the legs, torso, arms and finger joints, so that one slot means the same joint motion on every
robot (Section 3.2). Second, one model is trained jointly on these families in visual, action-conditioned and

Both roles are obtained from this one foundation, through one action interface. A single model predicts
video on held-out recordings of six robot families, with no robot-specific generator or action projection
(Section 5.1), and a single-arm Franka, absent from the robotics corpus, is controlled through the same shared
interface on CAL VIN and LIBERO. As a world model, GreenW AMattains top-tier trajectory, depth and
3D accuracy on WorldArena 1.0, ahead of every world–action model and video generator in our comparison,
including Motubrain [ 56] and FlowW AM-FiveAges [ 21] (Section 5.2). As a policy, it scores among the
top entries of the RoboTwin 2.0 leaderboard, reaches the highest zero-shot LIBERO-plus success among
the compared methods and is competitive on CAL VIN and LIBERO (Section 6). On a real humanoid,
it completes 95.6% of in-distribution instructions, against 88.9% for a policy adapted from its parent and
84.4% for 𝜋0.5, and a distilled single-step student answers 12.3 times faster than the unoptimized policy
(Sections 6.4 and 6.5).
Controlled comparisons (Section 7) identify what makes this work. First, robot specialization improves
world prediction and control together (Section 7.1). Adapted from GreenW AMrather than from its action-
trained parent, the world model raises trajectory accuracy on WorldArena by 3.0 points and lowers its video
prediction error by 23%. The policy predicts held-out RoboTwin frames more accurately and succeeds 5.4%
more often, or 8.4% with the action expert. Second, splitting an action expert off the generator at the start
2
of adaptation raises policy success by 3.3% (Section 7.2). Third, keeping world-model modes during policy
adaptation raises success in randomized scenes by 8.0% (Section 7.3).
Contributions. We make four contributions.
• Recipe. A shared anatomical action space and a data pipeline that adapt an action-trained world foun-
dation model to 20 robot families (Sections 3 and 4).
• One model, two roles. Joint training in seven visual, action-conditioned and world–action modes yields a
world model and, through an action expert split off the generator, a policy (Sections 3.3, 4.3, 6.1 and 6.2).
• What it brings and why. Results in both roles on WorldArena 1.0, RoboTwin 2.0, CAL VIN, RoboDojo,
LIBERO and LIBERO-plus, and controlled comparisons showing that robot specialization benefits both
roles (Sections 5 to 7).
• Real robot. Adaptation to a real humanoid and distillation into a single-step policy for real-time control
(Sections 6.4 and 6.5).

Robotics specialization of world foundations. Cosmos 3 already combines visual generation, forward
and inverse dynamics, and world–action modeling in a reasoner–generator architecture [ 58]. Its robot data,
however, cover six embodiments, with 81% of the hours from one dataset, and represent actions as end-
effector pose deltas with a gripper opening. GreenW AM retrains this foundation on measured joint and
Cartesian trajectories of 20 robot families, aligned with subtask video and text. Genie Envisioner likewise
connects a shared world foundation to prediction, control and simulation applications [ 47]. Alternative
data routes include human video with latent proxy actions in DreamDojo and generated robot experience in
GigaWorld-0 [25, 27]. Earlier work established action-conditioned video prediction as an interactive simulator
learned from diverse datasets [ 89], and V-JEPA 2 post-trains a latent action-conditioned world model for
robot planning [ 6].
Correspondences across robot bodies. Cross-embodiment policies face the same correspondence problem.
Open X-Embodiment pools demonstrations of 22 robots [ 60]; Octo is fine-tuned to new sensors and action
spaces [ 59], and CrossFormer controls robots for manipulation, navigation, locomotion and aviation without
manually aligning their observation or action spaces [ 22]. HPT maps the proprioception and vision of each
embodiment to tokens processed by a shared trunk [ 80], X-VLA adds embodiment-specific soft prompts [ 103],
UniAct learns a universal space of atomic actions [ 102], and RDT-1B defines a unified action space whose
coordinates keep their physical meaning [ 49]. Green-VLA uses a unified, embodiment-aware action interface
with which a single policy controls humanoids, mobile manipulators and fixed-base arms, and then adapts
this policy to the Green humanoid [ 5]. Among world models, Cosmos 3 represents camera and end-effector
motion together with gripper or fingertip state, using an input and output projection for each embodiment
3
domain [ 58]. GE-Sim 2.0 represents actions as action maps: the end-effector pose of each arm is projected
into every camera image with calibrated intrinsics and extrinsics, drawn as a marker whose shading gives the
gripper opening, and concatenated with the video latents [ 63]. Such maps are natural for a video generator
and simple to build, and they suit robots whose cameras are calibrated in every frame. For humanoids with
moving head and wrist cameras they require exact calibration of every view, and they cannot express finger
joints. GreenW AM instead passes the measured joint and Cartesian states through one unified projector
(Section 3.2) and still follows the commanded actions closely (Sections 5.1 and 5.2). GreenW AMuses explicit
slots and masks: its 79 anatomical slots cover legs, base, torso, head, arm joints and finger joints.

From world prediction to policy. Early approaches generate
a plan as video and extract the actions from it [ 23], or pretrain
on video and fine-tune for both video and action prediction [ 16].
UV A learns a joint video–action latent with decoupled decoding
heads [ 43], and UWM couples video and action diffusion so that
one model acts as a policy, forward dynamics, inverse dynam-
ics or video generator depending on the noise levels of the two
streams [ 104]; the modes of GreenW AM follow the same idea
across many robot bodies. Video-model priors support control
through several prediction interfaces: joint video–action genera-

in LingBot-V A, and visual planning followed by inverse dynamics
in GE-Act 2.0 [ 92, 42, 2]. As a policy, GreenW AMretains joint

a dedicated expert.
Policies built on video models also differ in whether actions
share the video backbone or receive an expert of their own, and in
how that expert is initialized. Motus pretrains an action expert
with latent actions, and ME-Dex inherits its video and action
experts [ 7, 99]. GreenW AMcopies its generator into an action
expert at the split, preserving the parent function before further optimization. Section 7.2 compares this
split with a shared tower from the same start.
Temporal context and simulation. W ALL-WM aligns captions, video and actions at the level of semantic
events [ 44], related to the complete-subtask atoms used in our data pipeline. Genie Envisioner and GE-
Sim 2.0 use sparse history for long rollouts, with GE-Sim 2.0 perturbing history during training to account
for generated-context errors [ 47, 63]. DreamZero updates its cached predictions with real observations during
closed-loop execution [ 92]. GreenW AMtakes four anchors at fixed positions of the history, each encoded
separately, and trains continuation with recorded history; Figure 10 shows its rollouts of more than 20 s on
WorldArena.
World simulators also support uses beyond video prediction. GigaWorld-1 studies agreement between
generated rollouts and real policy executions [ 28]; GE-Sim 2.0 predicts proprioception and evaluates task
completion in simulated rollouts [ 63]. W ALL-SS addresses long action-conditioned simulation with bounded
memory and alignment against drift [ 96], and the 1X World Model supports policy and checkpoint selec-
tion [ 1].
3 GreenWAM
GreenW AMmaps the states of different robots into shared anatomical coordinates and trains one generator
to predict video, robot motion or both; a policy is obtained by splitting an action expert off this genera-
tor (Section 6.1). Layout-specific codecs absorb the differences between native robot interfaces; Section 4
describes the data and the training.

3.1 Model Overview
We initialize GreenW AMfrom the released Cosmos 3 Edge 4B checkpoint [ 58]. Its mixture-of-transformers
backbone (Figure 3) couples a reasoner and a generator, with a causal video V AE and rectified-flow train-
ing [ 50]. Its robot actions, however, come from six embodiments and are represented as end-effector pose
deltas with a gripper opening (Figure 2); the robotics stage retrains the action modes on measured joint and
Cartesian states of 20 robot families, with the joints in a shared anatomical action space.
During the robotics stage the reasoner and the V AE stay frozen. The generator, the shared action
projections and the action-modality embeddings are trained. All modes retain the Cosmos 3 JSON prompt
format (Section 4.2).

3.2 Shared Anatomical Action Space
Robot datasets disagree on state layout, joint order, units, sign and zero conventions. Embodiment-specific
projectors accommodate these formats but leave the correspondence between their input coordinates implicit.
GreenW AMestablishes that correspondence before projection: layout-specific codecs map native channels to
79 anatomical coordinates, which then pass through one shared input and output projector (Figure 4). Thus
the left elbow occupies the same slot on every platform, while platform-specific conventions are handled by
the codec. All 20 families therefore share one action projector, and no robot has action parameters of its own.
This is a stricter design than one projection per embodiment domain, as in Cosmos 3 Edge, or end-effector
poses rendered into the camera images, as in GE-Sim 2.0 [ 63]. The vocabulary has four blocks:
• Legs (12).
• Body (23): base pose (7), base twist (6), torso (7) and head (3). Pose and twist are separate quantities.
• Arms (18).
• Hands (26): one open–closed coordinate per parallel gripper and per-finger coordinates for dexterous
hands, with auxiliary slots for a second gripper finger, encoders and finger abduction.
Layout-specific codecs select, reorder and normalize native channels into anatomical slots. Validity masks
exclude missing or unresolved coordinates from the loss. Correspondences are established from documented
channels, robot kinematics and measured trajectories; Section A.3 gives the details. New post-training
layouts use these same slots and shared projectors. For a layout ℓ with native state 𝑥, the codec assigns slot
𝑗 either a native channel 𝜋ℓ(𝑗) with a sign 𝜌ℓ𝑗 ∈ {−1, 1} and a zero offset 𝑜ℓ𝑗, or nothing:

3.4 Multi-View Observations and Temporal Context
Our corpus contains episodes with either one camera or three synchronized cameras. For three-view sources,
we compose the head and two wrist views offline into one video per contiguous kept segment (Figure 5). The
head view occupies approximately the top two thirds, with the wrist views below in a fixed Left | Right order.
Choosing among five 480p canvas buckets preserves the head-camera aspect ratio and keeps the aspect-ratio
change of every panel within 0.94–1.15; single-camera sources use the full frame. Camera roles are verified
per source by correlating view motion with left- and right-arm motion. Canvas sizes and temporal sampling
are specified in Section B.2.
The autoregressive modes condition each new block on sparse visual history, as in Genie Envisioner and
GE-Sim 2.0 [ 47, 63]. As in Genie Envisioner, the generator receives four RGB anchors; unlike its random
draws during training, ours sit at the same fixed positions in training and at inference: the start, one third,
two thirds and end of the available history on the effective video grid, so the last anchor is always the most
recent frame. Each anchor is encoded separately by the V AE; at the start of an episode the initial frame
is repeated. Training uses ground-truth history (teacher forcing); at inference the history is the generated
video. History may cross neighboring kept subtasks of the same episode and camera, and it is reset at
rejected gaps and episode boundaries. Each block carries its own caption or instruction.

From captions to instructions. The text pipeline produces a visual description and a prospective command
for each atom (Figure 6c and Figure 7). A video captioner (Qwen3.8-27B [ 64]) writes a structured record
one field per turn in a single dialogue, so each field is conditioned on those already written: subjects (the
robot and its effector), background, lighting, aesthetics, context, an attempt record, timed action phases, a
temporal caption and a consistency check. The source task label supplies the intended goal, and registry
facts fix the embodiment, effector type and real or simulated domain. Before the action phases, the captioner
records how far the attempt got, reading it from the final frame rather than from the label; later fields must
end where the clip ends, and a caption whose motion does not match the object and kind of action named
by the label is rejected. A second, text-only stage converts the action phases into a prospective command for
the same fragment. It preserves the observed motions, object references and phase order, including partial
or failed attempts, rather than completing an intended task that is not demonstrated. Using the source
label as the command would instead pair an unfinished attempt with a goal that the clip never reaches.
The conversion does not see native dataset instructions. Visual modes use the visual description; forward
dynamics and W AM use the derived command; inverse dynamics uses a fixed trajectory-recovery instruction.
Section B.3 details the captioner, the conversion, their checks and the final prompt format.

6 GreenWAM as a Policy
We adapt GreenW AMto control by predicting a future state trajectory together with video. Sections 6.1
and 6.2 describe how a policy is obtained: the action expert split off the generator and the post-training
common to all benchmarks, while each benchmark keeps its own demonstrations, command interface and
inference settings. Section 6.3 reports the four simulation benchmarks. Real-time control on the humanoid
benefits from a faster policy, so Section 6.4 distills the policy into a one-step student and measures its latency,
and Section 6.5 evaluates the teacher and the student on the robot.
6.1 Policy Architecture
In the robotics stage of GreenW AM, video and action tokens pass through one generator. For policy adap-
tation, action tokens move to a dedicated transformer while video tokens remain in the generator. Joint
attention connects the two streams at every layer, so action prediction retains access to visual dynamics
while the streams acquire separate parameters during adaptation. Related expert architectures differ in how
the expert is initialized [ 7, 99].
Let ℎ𝑧 and ℎ𝑞 be the video and action tokens entering a layer. The generator 𝐺 projects the video tokens
and the expert 𝐸 the action tokens, attention runs over both streams together, and each stream keeps its
own feed-forward block:
(𝑄, 𝐾, 𝑉 )𝑧 = 𝑊 𝐺 ℎ𝑧, (𝑄, 𝐾, 𝑉 ) 𝑞 = 𝑊 𝐸 ℎ𝑞, [ ̃ℎ𝑧; ̃ℎ𝑞] = Attn([𝑄𝑧; 𝑄𝑞], [𝐾𝑧; 𝐾𝑞], [𝑉 𝑧; 𝑉 𝑞]). (8)

6.2 Policy Post-Training
Policy adaptation connects joint video–state prediction to a target robot’s control loop. Each benchmark
specifies its demonstrations, camera layout and command interface; the common learning objective is the
W AM mode of GreenW AM(Equation ( 4); recipes in Section B.5).
Shared recipe. The policy receives a task instruction, the current camera canvas and the measured state
𝑞0, and predicts future video together with state offsets over horizon 𝐻. Training uses demonstrations of the
target robot, with measured future states as supervision. At inference, the predicted targets are decoded
relative to 𝑞0 (Equation ( 2)) into the robot’s native command representation. The controller executes a
prefix of the prediction, acquires a new observation and replans. The decoder and the execution interval are
specific to each application.
Appearance augmentation. Clean demonstrations show one appearance of each scene, and benchmarks
that randomize scenes test others. Two augmentations address this without touching the geometry. Back-
ground replacement: in half of the samples the static background (table, walls, floor), segmented by replaying
the demonstration in the simulator, is replaced by a procedural texture modulated by the original shading,
so that shadows are preserved; it needs the simulator for segmentation and is used on RoboTwin. Color
augmentation perturbs the colors of each camera image independently; it is applied to 80% of the samples
and adds no crops or rotations. Section 7.5 measures what background replacement brings.
The per-benchmark recipes, camera layouts, data volumes and execution settings are given in Section B.5.

6.4 Distillation for Real-Time Control
We distill the adapted policy into a one-step student with modality-specific consistency objectives, following
Flash-W AM [4] (Figure 14). The student keeps the teacher’s observations, state representation and joint
video–action outputs; only its sampling changes at deployment. We initialize a frozen teacher, a trainable
student and an EMA target student from the adapted policy. Unlike Flash-W AM, which uses separate noise
schedules for video and actions, video latents and the action chunk share one noise level. The teacher steps
between neighboring levels of a shared schedule of 4 noise levels, from a noisier sample to a less noisy one.
The teacher makes two passes, with the instruction and with an empty prompt, and applies guidance to
video (scale 3–4) but not to actions; the student learns to produce a consistent clean prediction at both
points, with the lower-noise target supplied by the EMA student and detached from the gradient.
Video and action targets use separate consistency maps. The video map uses noise-dependent skip and
output coeﬀicients, while the action map directly estimates the clean state target as 𝑥𝜎 − 𝜎 ̂ 𝑣𝜃. An action
flow-matching term, which Flash-W AM uses only in a regularized baseline, keeps the student anchored to
the demonstration targets:

7 Empirical Analysis
We test three choices in adapting the shared foundation: robot-specialized initialization, a separate action
expert and the retention of world-model modes during policy adaptation. Each comparison holds the down-
stream data and the adaptation budget fixed (Figure 18). We then examine how individual scene changes
affect the policy and what appearance augmentation recovers; evaluation details are given in Section C.5.

7.2 Coupling the World Model to Action
Splitting the action expert at the start of adaptation raises policy success while preserving the initial pre-
dictions. During policy adaptation, GreenW AM moves action tokens into an expert (Equation ( 8)) that
starts as an exact copy of the generator tower, so the split changes the trainable parameterization without
changing the initial predictions (Section 6.1). Both runs therefore start from the same function.
Trained for 3 000 steps from GreenW AMwith the recipe of Section 7.1, the policy with the expert succeeds
3.3% more often on average than the one that keeps the shared generator, 2.8% in clean and 3.7% in
randomized scenes. It raises success on 28 tasks and lowers it on 11 in clean scenes, and on 22 and 7 in
randomized scenes. The gain comes with almost equal validation losses (1.503 with the expert and 1.510
without).

A review of 20 randomly drawn failures in each setting shows how they arise. In clean scenes, 19 of them

7.5 Robustness to Scene Randomization
Randomization changes appearance and geometry at once, so a single success rate does not show which change
matters most. We therefore vary one axis at a time (Figure 24) and then test appearance augmentation;
Table 3 lists the clean and randomized success of other methods with their training data.
Individual scene changes. The single-axis tests use seeds outside the oﬀicial set. Background textures
and table height cause the largest drops, 12% and 8% below the clean reference; distractors and lighting
change success by less than 1%, and unseen instructions do not lower it. A new background changes only the
image, which augmentation can target. A table 3 cm higher or lower changes the trajectory the arm must
follow, and the augmentations used here change observations while keeping the recorded states and actions,
so they add no demonstrations of such motions.
Trajectory accuracy and replanning. Execution is sensitive to small errors: replaying expert trajectories
with joint noise of 0.05 rad succeeds in 80% of trials, and with 0.1 rad in 30%. Executing 8 instead of 16
predicted steps before replanning lowers clean success by 14.8%.
Appearance augmentation. Background replacement targets the costliest appearance change: in a paired
comparison on tasks chosen before training, it raises randomized success by 13.1% and leaves clean success
unchanged.
8 Discussion and Limitations
One foundation for both roles. A single robotics checkpoint yields both a world model and policies. The
controlled comparisons connect the two roles: robot specialization improves forward prediction and policy
25
success at the same budget, and the policy that predicts held-out frames more accurately also succeeds more
often.
World modeling as part of control. Keeping forward dynamics and image-to-video next to W AM during
adaptation raises randomized RoboTwin success by 8.0%, although both variants predict video. Decoding
the policy’s own predictions shows the same link inside one model: out of distribution, success follows the
accuracy of the imagined scene, which is where retaining world-model modes pays off (Section 7.4).
One action space for all bodies. One projector serves all 20 families, so a new robot needs a codec rather
than new action parameters (Section A.3). The single-arm Franka of CAL VIN and LIBERO, absent from
the robotics corpus, enters through the same interface and reaches 4.64 on CAL VIN and 97.4% on LIBERO.
Limitations. The policies remain sensitive to changes in task geometry, such as table height on RoboTwin
or the robot’s initial configuration on LIBERO-plus: the augmentations used here change observations but
add no new motions. Long rollouts continue from the model’s own frames, so prediction error accumulates
from block to block. Each benchmark is reached through its own post-training, and the real-robot evaluation
covers one humanoid and one packing task.
Outlook. Natural next steps are running the distilled single-step policy on the robot’s onboard computer,
transfer to bodies that neither the parent nor the robotics stage has seen, motion-level augmentation for
robustness to task geometry, and the use of the world-model role to evaluate policies in imagined rollouts.
9 Conclusion
We asked whether one robot world model can serve both as a world model and as a policy across different
bodies, and what makes this work. Adapted from Cosmos 3 Edge to 20 robot families through a shared
anatomical action space and joint video–action training, one foundation yields a world model with top-tier
trajectory, depth and 3D accuracy on WorldArena 1.0, ahead of all compared world–action models and video
generators, and policies that score among the top entries of the RoboTwin 2.0 leaderboard, are competitive
on CAL VIN and LIBERO and, on a real humanoid, succeed more often than policies adapted from 𝜋0.5 and
from Cosmos 3 Edge. Controlled comparisons identify three choices that make this work: robot specialization,
which improves both roles together, an action expert split off the generator, and world-model modes kept
during policy adaptation. A distilled one-step student answers at 28.3 Hz and keeps its lead over 𝜋0.5 on the
robot. We release the weights of the RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO and CAL VIN policies and of the WorldArena
world model, together with the code that evaluates them. Because a new robot needs a codec rather than
new action parameters, the next steps are bodies unseen in training and more reliable long-horizon prediction
and control under changes in task geometry.

A.2 Corpus Admission and Quality Filtering
Sources. Each source is converted to one LeRobot layout and registered with its camera roles, state layout
and three static facts that the captioner later receives: embodiment, end-effector type and real or simulated
domain. Camera roles and the meaning of state and action columns are established from metadata, sampled
tables and video rather than from column names. A video stream needs more than 10 FPS and at least 448
pixels on the short side; depth and infrared streams are not used. Failed and recovery demonstrations are
kept, since for a world model they are valid physical rollouts.

only when no finer annotation exists; missing or overlapping boundaries are marked unresolved rather than

B.2 Temporal Sampling and Prediction Targets
Physical time. We retain each source’s physical timing and cap the effective video rate at 30 FPS. A
source recorded at 𝑓𝑠 is read with stride max (1, ⌈𝑓𝑠/30⌉): 15, 20 and 30 FPS are retained, while 50 FPS
becomes 25 FPS. Video frames and measured states are selected on the same time grid, and the effective
rate is passed to the model. This preserves motion speed in physical time; joint positions and displacements
are not multiplied by the sampling-rate ratio. Three-view sources use the 480p canvas buckets 640 ×640,
736×544, 544 ×736, 832 ×480 and 480 ×832 according to the head-camera aspect ratio.
Whole subtasks. A fixed-length clip can cut an action in the middle and pair an incomplete grasp with
the description of the whole grasp. Text-to-video, image-to-video, forward dynamics and inverse dynamics
therefore use a complete prepared subtask rather than a fixed-length clip. In the autoregressive modes one
block is one whole subtask; a following block is added only if it fits completely, and the last block is never
truncated. The autoregressive budget is expressed in physical time, up to 30 s of future video, so the number
of predicted frames follows the frame rate of the source.
History anchors. Let 𝑎 be the start of the contiguous kept history and 𝑏 the last observed boundary, both
in native-frame indices, and let 𝑠 be the stride on the effective video grid. With 𝑁 = ⌊(𝑏 − 𝑎)/𝑠⌋ , the four
RGB anchors are

B.3 Structured Captions and Commands
A visual description records what is visible, including the scene and the observed outcome; an action instruc-
tion specifies what the robot should do during the same fragment. We keep both for every atom instead of
treating a retrospective caption as a policy command.
Captioner. A vision-language model (Qwen3.8-27B [ 64] with FP8 weights) captions the admission-camera
clip of one atom. It also receives the clip duration, the source subtask label and three registry facts: embod-
iment (humanoid or bimanual platform), end effector (dexterous hand or two-finger gripper) and domain
(real or simulation). The label supplies the goal and the target object, while only the frames establish how
far the attempt got. The registry facts are taken as given, so a cropped or occluded effector does not change
the described embodiment.
Field-by-field generation. The captioner writes one field per turn in a single dialogue, and each answer
is appended to the context before the next request (Table 6). Later fields must therefore agree with earlier
ones, and one object keeps one name and one color throughout the record. Action phases start at 0:00,
stay ordered and contiguous and end at the clip duration. A shared instruction block fixes the vocabulary:
actor-relative left and right, the registry’s effector term, no camera language, no hedging and no contact
detail that the view cannot resolve. An attempt record, written before the action phases, states the end state,
whether the target moved and where the effector ends; a clip that ends mid-motion then receives phases and
a narrative that also end mid-motion.

WorldArena 1.0. The world model starts from GreenW AMand is adapted for 10 000 steps with a global
batch of 64, a peak learning rate of 1.41 × 10−5 after 500 warm-up steps and a cosine decay to 0.1 of the peak.
It is trained on the clean RoboTwin 2.0 demonstrations, whose ARX5 arms are those of the benchmark,
rendered as a single head-camera canvas of 736 ×544 and cut into blocks of at most 15 s. Only forward
dynamics with history is trained, and the text prompt is always empty, as in the action variant of the
benchmark. At evaluation every block, including the first, is generated in this mode from four anchors of the
history, which for the first block repeat the recorded first frame; sampling uses 35 steps without guidance.
LIBERO. The LIBERO policy is adapted from GreenW AM with the action expert in stages, with ap-
pearance augmentation, a random perspective distortion of the third-person camera image and paraphrased
instructions. No LIBERO-plus episode is used in training, and the same policy is evaluated on both bench-
marks.
RoboDojo. The policy follows the oﬀicial protocol: it is trained on the oﬀicial training set and com-
pared with the entries of the oﬀicial leaderboard (Section C.1). It uses the generator–expert architecture
of GreenW AMwith attention restricted by stream: the history attends to the instruction and to itself, the
current frame to the instruction, the history and itself, the state to the current frame and itself, and the
actions to the generated video, the state and themselves. The history is pinned after the instruction, and the
current frame, the generated video and the actions are placed at their time offsets from it. Training runs for
37
60 000 steps with a global batch of 352, a learning rate warmed up over 3 000 steps to 8×10 −5 and decayed by
a cosine to 10−6, weight decay 0.01, an exponential moving average of the weights and quantile-normalized
actions; a fifth of the training examples are image-to-video.
CAL VIN. The CAL VIN policy is trained on the ABC training split only (Section C). Its single Franka arm
enters GreenW AMthrough the Cartesian end-effector slots with per-frame SE(3) offsets and the absolute
initial pose as the current state. At evaluation the policy predicts 20 steps, samples them with 4 UniPC
steps, a timestep shift of 3 and guidance 1.5, and executes the first 10 before replanning.
Green humanoid. The comparison uses three initializations: the public 𝜋0.5 base checkpoint [61], Cosmos 3
Edge, and GreenW AM. They are adapted on the same Green demonstration split. The distilled policy starts
from the reported GreenW AMteacher and uses only the training split for the consistency and demonstration
losses. Its consistency losses use a masked pseudo-Huber distance [ 72], with unavailable action coordinates
and clean conditions excluded; the loss weights are (1, 1, 0.01) and the EMA decay is 0.995. Only the online
student is optimized; the teacher and the EMA target are used only during training. Validation episodes
select checkpoints and inference settings; neither ID test episodes nor OOD trials are used for selection.
Each policy retains its own observation preprocessing, action representation and training recipe, with a
common set of camera streams and controlled joints at the robot interface. Normalization statistics are fitted
on training data.

C.3 Latency Benchmark
We benchmark inference latency on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 with FlashAttention-2 (Table 10). The
video branch works on three latent frames: one conditioning frame and two generated frames, which decode
into an eight-frame prediction at 10 Hz. Each request also conditions on the proprioceptive state and a
128-token instruction. To isolate the cost of the model, the observation and the state stay fixed within an
episode, and the instruction changes once, at its start. Median latency is the steady-state latency after
the instruction-dependent quantities have been cached; time to first action (TTA) is the latency of the first
request after a new instruction and includes building that cache. Each value is computed over 100 episodes,
and the rate is 1000 divided by the latency in milliseconds.

The optimizations are applied in turn. Caching reuses the instruction and vision key–value states across

instruction cache. Once caching is enabled, a new instruction adds 11–20 ms to the first request, and 13.4 ms
for the distilled policy.
